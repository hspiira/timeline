Preface
It is my pleasure and honour to inform our stakeholders about the State of Higher Education in 
Uganda through this annual publication. This report also serves to guide Higher Education (HE) 
policy direction.
In the Academic Year 2019/20, the HE Sub-Sector, like all other sector in the economy met many 
challenges presented by Covid-19 pandemic. As a result, the HE capacity indicators declined in most 
of the areas that had earlier registered improvements. Several HEIs failed to conclude their study 
programmes within the year or even graduate students and many are still struggling to sustain 
their operations. The gross effects of Covid-19 pandemic demonstrated that most HEIs depend 
on tuition fees alone, which proved unsustainable. Almost all HEIs hardly realised their planned 
budgets and operated on emergency budgets that integrated Covid-19 pandemic issues.
In an attempt to sustain HEIs teaching and learning, NCHE approved emergency ODeL guidelines. 
The Universities and Other Degree Awarding Institutions (ODAIs) were quick to adjust and 
implemented these guidelines accordingly. The Other Tertiary Institutions (OTIs) however, could 
not support ODeL because they lacked infrastructure and required skills. Sadly, about ten (10) 
OTIs ceased to operate during this period due to many challenges.
Whereas eligible population to study in HEIs grew by about 3.1% according to population growth 
estimates, the overall enrolment declined by 3.8%. These two forces negatively affected the 
Growth Enrolment Ratio (GER) from an average of 6.8% over the last five years to 5.3% in 2019/20. 
Generally, the statistics showed 1.1% and 3.6% growth in enrolment in Universities and ODAIs, 
respectively while OTIs registered 17.8% decline in enrolment. Overall, the total enrolment declined 
from 275,254 in 2018/19 to 264,908 in 2019/20 representing total decline of 3.8%.
There was a positive trend towards STEM both in number of programmes and enrolment across 
public and private HEIs. The HEIs however, are still grappling with inadequacy of equipment, 
laboratories and qualified academic staff at PhD levels of education. The experience of Covid-19 
pandemic should act as revelation of challenges that threaten current and potential future gains 
in HE. These issues require concerted effort from all stakeholders if the country is to realize the 
desired numbers and quality of human resource to achieve the goals of NDPIII and the long term 
Uganda Vision 2040.
I wish to thank the Directorate of ICT, Research and Innovation for their tireless efforts to have this 
annual report published amidst all operational challenges exacerbated by Covid-19 pandemic. In 
the same spirit, on behalf of NCHE I wish to thank all HEIs for providing the relevant data for the 
production of this report. NCHE will disseminate the emerging issues raised in this report as per 
standard practice. It is our hope that all our strategic partners in higher education will contribute 
towards improving these critical success indicators in the higher education subsector.
Professor Mary J. N. Okwakol
Executive Director, NCHE

Definitions
Gross Enrolment Ratio
The number of students enrolled at a level of education, regardless of age, as percentage of 
the population of official school age for that level.
Net Enrolment Ratio
The number of students enrolled at a level of education who are of official school going age 
for that level, as a percentage of the official school age population for that level.
Gross Tertiary Science Enrolment Ratio
The number of students enrolled in tertiary education in science, regardless of age, as a 
percentage of the population of the relevant age range. In this context, science refers to 
physical, natural, health, mathematical, engineering, computer, agricultural, architectural, 
forestry and fisheries sciences. It does not include social or metaphysical sciences.

Executive Summary
1.0 Introduction
The State 
of Higher Education and Training in Uganda is an annual publication by the 
National Council for Higher Education (NCHE) that provides a status of Higher Education 
Institutions (HEIs) and training in Ugandan Higher Education subsector. Experience shows 
that not all admitted students take up their places at HEIs and others dropout during the 
course of study. Equally, HEIs implement many development and research activities 
during the academic year that reflect its growth. Therefore, to obtain accurate status for 
the year, NCHE collects this data on a concluded academic year. The reports from HEIs 
and the media all show that in Academic Year 2019/20 the higher education sub sector like 
other sectors was grossly affected by Covid-19 pandemic. Several HEIs failed to conclude 
their study programmes or even graduate their students and many are still struggling to 
sustain their status. Despite these challenges, the NCHE was able to obtain representative 
data to support decision-making.
1.1 The Higher Education Sub-Sectors at a glance
“The State of Higher Education and Training” is an annual publication that 
provides 
a snapshot on the Ugandan higher education sub-sector. Although previous reports 
observed continued growth of the subsector in terms of student enrolments & GER and the 
number of institutions, 2019/20 registered a decline in most of higher education statistical 
indicators. The effects of Covid-19 grossly affected the operations and performance of 
higher education institutions in delivery of higher education. Whereas HEIs continued 
to depend on tuition fees as a major source of income, Covid-19 pandemic demonstrated 
that depending on tuition alone could not sustain institutional operations. Some higher 
education institutions especially those under Other Tertiary Institutions (OTIs) could not 
cope with the demands of the “new norm” that requires blended ODeL and traditional 
mode of deliveries because they lacked infrastructure and required skills. Generally, OTIs 
tended to be less responsive to meetings, workshops and training conducted online.
The state and other stakeholders must take an active role in addressing the problem of 
enabling affordable ICT infrastructure, skills development and access to internet for 
educational. Other Tertiary Institutions produce middle cadres required in the productive 
sectors of the economy yet they are failing to cope with the “new norm”. Government needs 
to design ways to support these institutions to embrace ICT usage for educational purposes. 
NCHE need to urge government to speed up the positioning of OTIs and cleaning up the 
conflicting regulations that allow OTIs to “sit on the fence.”

nine (9), private universities were forty-four (44) and other degree awarding institutions 
increased to eleven (11) and the rest of the institutions were OTIs.
NCHE concluded the closure of two universities Kayiwa International University and 
Nsaka University due to non-compliance issues while eight OTIs ceased to operate due to

1.3 Enrolment
In 2019/20 student enrolment decreased 
275,254 in 2018/19 to 264,908 in 2019/20; 
representing a decline of 3.8%. The Ugandans students alone reduced from 254,930 to 
244,927 (3.9%) while foreign student reduced from 20,324 to 19,981 (1.7%). Enrolment at 
the university level increased from 192,346 in 2018/19 to 194,519 in 2019/20 (1.1%) while 
ODAIs increased by 3.6%. The OTI enrolment experienced a greater shock from 72,464 in 
2018/19 to 59,567 in 2019/20, representing 17.8% decline.
1.3.1 Gross Enrolment Ratio (GER)
The GER dropped from 6.8% in 2018/19 to 5.3% in 2019/20 caused by increasing population 
(age group eligible 
for tertiary education) coupled with drastic decline in enrolment 
especially for OTIs. Whereas enrolment in universities and ODAIs grew by 1.1% and 3.6% 
respectively, the enrolment for OTIs declined by 17.8%, which drastically affected the ratio. 
The GER of 5.3% far below the current sub-Saharan Africa average of 9.4% and the global 
average of 38%.
All subsectors of education need to adjust quickly to the new norm so that the ratio can 
improve more than the level we had attained. This may only be possible if government 
with support from all stakeholders designs and implements strategies to increase access 
and adoption of ICT for learning.

1.4.3 Academic Staff availability
Higher

This is acceptable according to NCHE quality assurance framework.

In 2019/20, NCHE received 1,004 programmes for review but assessed

diplomas and higher education certificates, which were 14% and 12% respectively.
1.6.0 Research and Innovation
HEIs reported less research but 
from ODeL monitoring reports, a majority of HEIs 
including all public and private universities experienced intermittent donor support on 
research during Covid-19 pandemic crisis. However, universities ventured into developing 
sanitizers, sanitizer dispensing machines (manual and digital solar powered). Some 
attempted to develop cure for Covid-19 and ventilators to aid Covid-19 patients. Generally, 
HEIs played an active role in combating Covid-19 but were all constrained by inadequate 
research funds. All the reported research was from the universities.
1.6.1 Infrastructure
Infrastructure here includes lecture rooms/theatres, Library space, laboratories, staff and 
office space.
1.6.2 Lecture Space
The total lecture space 
reported in 2019/20 was 335,922 representing a negligible 
improvement of 0.2%. The space per student improved slightly from 1.22m2 per student in 
2018/19 to 1.27m2 in 2019/20 because of the decline in student numbers.
1.6.3 Library Space
The total library space increased slightly by about 4.7% because of completed library 
expansion projects 
especially in some public HEIs that benefitted from AfDB. This increase 
however, had a negligible effect on overall ratio of library space per student from 0.34m2 in 
2018/19 to 0.37m2 in 2019/20.

1.6.5 Office space
Although HEIs 
have made effort to provide space for administrators and guild offices, 
HEIs ignore academic staff yet that space is vital for planning and for student consultations.

1.7.0  Information and Communication Technologies (ICT)
In 2019/20, the number of computers 
accessed by students was 19,213 while the overall 
number of functional computers in HEIs reported was 20,147 less by 5,298 reported in 
2018/19. The computers accessible to students (students + shared) were 19,213 which gives 
access ratio of 14 students per computer.
1.7.1 Internet connectivity
Internet connectivity and availability 
of e-resources eases pressure on the physical space 
for the library; however, bandwidth, access to internet and e-resources is still wanting. 
NCHE encourages HEIs to collaborate with e-resource and Internet service providers to 
increase access to e-resources.
1.7.2 Open Distance e-Learning (ODeL)
In 2019/20, 
following closure of HEIs, NCHE developed an emergency ODeL programme 
to enable HEIs complete the academic year. Whereas this required well-developed ICT 
systems, well-equipped computer laboratories, robust servers and adequate bandwidth to 
withstand data transaction, a majority of HEIs were ill equipped to handle these demands. 
The HEIs did not provide comprehensive data on this however; the ODeL monitoring 
reports showed that at the universities level, almost all have robust servers, weak monitoring 
systems and inadequacy of computers. The bandwidth remains inadequate to handle the 
demands. Generally, staff were ill prepared to adopt ODeL mode of delivery because skills 
gaps. The HEIs adopted blended learning rather than pure ODeL. HEIs taught practicum 
areas physically while observing SOPs. The OTIs could not cope with ODeL requirements 
and have lagged behind. They lack equipment & facilities and face scarcity of skills to 
conduct ODeL.
1.8 Books
NCHE recommends 
that every HEIs must have a physical library and a stock of hard copy 
textbooks in their respective institutions. This is because bandwidth, access to; computers, 
internet, e-resources, and a conducive reading environment for students is still wanting. 
Many student do not have access to computers and dwell in noisy neighbourhoods that are 
not conducive for study. In 2019/20, the number of hard copy textbooks dropped by 17% 
from 4,413,992 to 3,791,364. Other than theological colleges, other OTIs are operating below 
acceptable levels according to NCHE quality assurance indicators. The student-book ratio 
irrespective of the titles was 14 books per student.

The data obtained from a few HEIs that obliged show

1.10 Key issues and suggested recommendations for consideration
This subsection 
provides extract of key issues that require NCHE and government 
mitigation. These include outstanding challenges, gaps identified in 2019/20 report and 
corresponding recommendations.
1.10.1  Governance, regulatory framework and funding
HEIs 
need to diversify sources of funding minimize dependence on donor and tuition 
fees. OTIs teach BTVET and programmes NCHE accredited however, there is flexibility 
of leaning on a softer side. NCHE needs to expedite issues of overlapping regulatory 
framework.
The general observation is that there is need for HEIs to diversify sources of funding and 
improve institutional governance. As a regulator, NCHE should devise ways to increase 
access and sustain QA standards. Possibly increase monitoring and feedback and mentoring 
mechanisms to minimize HEIs closure.
1.10.2  Decline in enrolment and GER
The enrolment in universities and ODAIs grew by 1.1% and 3.6% respectively, however, 
there was a drastic decline in OTI enrolment and some even ceased to operate. The majority 
of OTIs failed to adapt the “new norm” under Covid-19 due to lack of infrastructure and 
skill required for ODeL mode of delivery of training and learning, which limited access to 
higher education during that period. Furthermore, most OTI budgets were funded using 
tuition fees. The decline in enrolment meant loss of funds, which consequently affected 
sustainability.
Additionally, rapid population growth projected at 3.1% for age group eligible for tertiary 
education increased the number in the age group not accessing higher education hence, 
negatively contributed to the drastic decline in GER. There could be other reasons but 
certainly, dependence on tuition and effects of Covid-19 influenced this decline.
1.10.3 Inadequate staff and low staff development at higher lev
els
Inadequate PhD holders and low levels of capacity building. NCHE to develop staff  
development policy and advocate for staff development across public and private 
institutions, after all private players are complementing the government role in provision of 
education and training. The other option is to advocate for staff development fund from 
government but also lobby key stakeholders to support HEIs in capacity building.

1.10.4  Low investment in STEM programmes
If government is to achieve the desired goal in Science, Technology, Engineering, 
Mathematics and Innovation (STEMI) as envisaged in Uganda Vision 2040, then policy 
makers should endeavour to strengthen support given to both public and private HEIs 
teaching science programmes without discrimination. Government needs to intensify its 
effort to strengthen science and technology to increase the gains on science enrolment.
Government need to invest more resources to develop the laboratories, provide equipment 
and train human resource across all levels of education. The primary and secondary 
subsectors feed tertiary levels therefore, there should be a deliberate effort to develop 
STEM at all levels of education.
As a policy measure, government could identify and strengthen higher education institutions 
teaching science based programmes irrespective of public /private ownership in effort to 
strengthen STEM.
1.10.5  Inadequate ICT resources, Internet access and slow adoption of ODeL mode of 
delivery
There 
was slow adoption of ICT for teaching and learning, inadequate bandwidth, ICT 
infrastructure and skills required for ODeL. Many OTIs failed to adopt ODeL while other 
ceased to operate even when government lifted lockdown. NCHE should pursue options 
of developing a stock of shared resources for example; developing common ODeL facility 
or platform in which NCHE can share specialized and scarce human resources hosted at 
NCHE using facilities such as video conferencing and other shared platforms.
Government needs collaborate with Internet Service Providers to increase internet access 
and make ICT equipment & bandwidth affordable for education purposes. There is need to 
enforce on ICT pedagogical skills across all levels of education.
Perhaps government should develop common resources to implement ODel in OTIs. This 
could improve if internet and computer access is improved and every student can access 
e-resources. HEIs and planners should rethink of alternative sustainable sources of funding 
if higher education is to stay sustainable.
1.10.6 Low research output and research fund
NCHE needs to develop close 
working relationship (including MoUs) with Uganda 
National Council of Science & Technology and other partners to ensure availability of and 
access to research fund. This will improve regulation of research in HEIs besides access to 
resources.

1.1  Methodology
1.2 Introduction
National Council for Higher Education (NCHE) cannot physically monitor all HEIs within 
a year given financial and human resource constraints. To address this, NCHE has continued 
collecting and publishing information on higher education statistics annually despite the 
limited funding. This information provides flagship on how HEIs are adhering or deviation 
from the quality assurance framework. This report provides statistics for all key indicators 
in higher education subsector.
The NCHE largely uses the approach of sending questionnaires to responsible officers at 
institutional level prior to the physical visits. NCHE liaises with focal persons identified in 
each higher education institution (HEI) who then play a central coordination role within 
their respective HEI. These officers provide centrally merged data from different schools, 
faculties, departments and units within the institution and return it to the NCHE Secretariat.
The year 2019/20 particularly faced many challenges associated with Covid-19; as a result, 
some of the key coordinating staff got infected by or succumbed to Covid-19. Secondly, a 
number of institutions still have challenges with record keeping which causes unnecessary 
delays. Some institutions do not comply, while some of questionnaire returned were 
incomplete and with inconsistent data. The technical staff addressed these challenges 
through follow up using telephone calls, email communication or physical visit to the 
concerned HEI.
1.3 Methods of Data Collection
Like in previous years, NCHE uses blended methods of data collection. The technical 
staff sent data collection instruments for 2019/20 to institutions in advance and later, data 
collectors made field follow-up visits to retrieve these tools and to validate data provided. 
This also provides an opportunity to guide officers who had challenges filling some sections 
of the questionnaires. Other sources of data included annual reports and monitoring 
reports from these institutions. The NCHE developed an IMIS but the HEIs are rather slow 
in adopting it than envisaged due to challenges in ICT and internet access. It is our hope that 
once adopted, HEIs will be able to submit timely data. The key data sources are;
1.3.1 Primary Sources with Self-Administered Structured Questionnaire
NCHE sends the questionnaires by e-mail together with instructions for filling them. 
Institutions can also access the tools through NCHE website. The technical staff review 
these tools regularly to accommodate emerging issues and remove obsolete content. It is 
a requirement that the completed questionnaires filled by the focal persons and the head 
of the HEI must endorse the filled tool before submission to NCHE to ensure authenticity 
of the data. The scheduled submission period has always been from June to September 
for the concluded academic year; however, 2019/20 was a unique year in which many 
HEIs did not conclude the academic year. This was after presidential directive to close 
all educational institutions due to Covid-19 pandemic. NCHE suspended data collection 
until official reopening of HEIs. Even when the president lifted the lockdown, many HEIs 
remained closed due to the pandemic shock.

categorizes the higher education institutions in Uganda into three broad categories. These are 
Universities, Other Degree Awarding Institutions (ODAIs) and Other Tertiary Institutions

programmes in specialized areas as assessed by NCHE. The OTIs are largely diploma 
awarding HEIs, which comprise of mainly business, management & social development, 
technical, agriculture, and teacher training among others.

2.5 Pathways to access higher education in Uganda 2019/20
The pathways to access higher education have not changed since 2014. The Quality 
Assurance Framework for Universities and the Licensing Process for Higher Education 
Institutions, January 2014 prescribes alternative avenues for entry to programmes in 
Ugandan higher education system that every institution must adhere. These requirements 
are specified for degree and diploma and postgraduate programmes.
The minimum entry requirements for the degree programmes include:
a) Direct entry 
from schools: Uganda Certificate of Education (UCE) with at least five 
(5) passes and two principal passes at Uganda Advanced Certificate of Education 
(UACE) or its equivalent.
b) Mature age: The candidate must be 25 years of age and abov
e and must have passed 
the mature age entry examinations with at least 50% mark. The National Council for 
Higher Education must approve the institutions that conduct mature age examinations.
c) Diploma obtained 
at a credit or distinction level in a relevant field from a recognized 
institution; or
d) Higher Education 
Certificate- abridging course for Advanced Level Equivalent. It is 
a requirement that National Council for Higher Education must have accredited the 
bridging course before any HEI administers it to prospective students.
The minimum requirements for admission to a diploma programme include:
a) Direct entry 
from schools: Uganda Certificate of Education (UCE) with at least five (5) 
passes; and
b) Uganda Adv
anced Certificate of Education (UACE) with one (1) principal pass and 
two subsidiaries passes obtained at the same sitting or its equivalent.
Lastly, for postgraduate programmes the minimum admission requirements are;
a) For master’s degree, a bachelor
’s degree or its equivalent; and
b) For a doctoral degree, a master’s degree or its equiv
alent.
Over the past years of operations, NCHE observed that some HEIs try to evade admission 
requirement and admit students who do not meet the minimum admission requirements. 
These institutions have had to pay dearly for their actions, the worst case scenarios were 
a revocation of operating license. NCHE advises HEIs to desist from this temptation and 
stick to the stipulated admission guidelines.

3.1  The Gross Enrolment Ratio
The Gross Enrolment Ratio (GER) measures the number of students enrolled in HEIs 
regardless of age as a percentage of the official school going age group corresponding 
to this level of education. The population eligible for tertiary education based on 2019 
population projections was approximately 4.984 million Ugandans. This put the GER in 
2019/20 at approximately 5.3%, which is lower than 6.8% achieved in 2018/19. Compared 
to the current sub-Saharan Africa average of 9.4% and the global average of 38% out GER is 
far below the reported averages. This decline was partially due to the disruptions caused by 
Covid-19 pandemic and rapid population growth for the age group. Figure 4.2 also shows 
that there has been a declining growth rate of enrolment associated with other factors over 
the past decade. Although the GER has been stable around 6.8 average, 2019/20 experienced 
a shock that pushed enrolment below the going average. This decline is not a surprise given the 
country’s projected population growth rate of about 3.1% and a registered decline in enrolment of 
3.9%. This decline in GER is a threat to the positive strides that the country had achieved in the past 
few years.

4.0  Academic Programmes
4.1  The general Picture
The National Council for Higher Education requires that HEIs must be accredit all 
programmes they teach at respective institutions as mandated by the Universities and Other 
Tertiary Institutions Act 2001(as amended). This includes the professional programmes 
that NCHE accredits in consultation with the professional bodies. Once accredited the PhD 
programmes remain valid for a period of 10 years while masters, bachelors and diploma 
programmes will be valid for only five years. NCHE requires that all HEIs must review 
and submit their programmes for re-accreditation before expiry date to avoid teaching 
expired or unaccredited programmes. The accreditation process at NCHE involves a series 
of stages including desk reviews by NCHE staff and reviews by experts in respective fields 
of study. The last stage involves physical verification of institutional facilities and staff  
aligned for the proposed programmes. These stages help minimize programme failure 
rates but most importantly, that programmes are aligned with the National Development 
Plans (NDP) of the country and going concerns globally.
On a sad note, some HEIs have been found teaching expired programmes and in some 
cases completely unaccredited programmes. Some of the affected HEIs have had their 
operation licenses revoked and acquired qualifications cancelled. All stakeholders should 
be aware that teaching unaccredited programmes is a criminal offence designed to fleece 
uninformed beneficiaries for private gains. NCHE encourages all stakeholders to verify the 
validity of the programme applied for or seek guidance from NCHE when in doubt.
In the year 2019/20 alone, NCHE received 1,004 programmes for review but assessed only 
819. Out of the assessed programmes, only 414 representing 46.5% fulfilled the minimum 
set criteria for respective programmes (Table 5.1).

and community engagement at HEIs. The quality of academic staff greatly influences 
institutional ability to achieve its goals of excellence in academic, research and innovations.

university and the necessary support provided to academic staff influences the level of 
creation of new knowledge by the university. NCHE developed a Statutory Instrument 
No. 50 of 2010 that specifies required qualifications of academic staff for every level.

6.3 Library Space
The total library space increased slightly from 94,152m2 in 2018/19 to 98,576m2 in 2019/20,

establish laboratories. As a policy measure, government could identify and strengthen 
higher education institutions teaching science based programmes irrespective of public /
private ownership in effort to strengthen STEM.
6.5   Space

staff. Although HEIs make effort to provide space for administrators and guild offices, 
they tend to ignore academic staff yet that space is vital for planning and for student 
consultations. Staff without office space will often find difficulties in conducting research 
and student supervision. In many institutions, staff members including top manager are 
share small office space, which limits student consultations. This needs improvement for 
all categories of staff.

7.0   Education Equipment, Books, Materials and Other Facilities
7.1  Introduction
This subsection summarises findings on equipment, books materials and other facilities 
available in HEIs to facilitate delivery of higher education. These include computers, books, 
laboratory/workshop materials and other facilities. Equipment tend to get obsolete while 
materials tend to expire if over stocked. HEIs and planners also need to match student 
numbers with the required education facilities. In particular, given the “new norm” under 
Covid-19, HEIs need to monitor access to computer & e-libraries, and internet availability 
as they greatly influence the ability of institutions to deliver quality higher education under 
new norm.

this new norm of Covid-19 pandemic. Data on students’ personal computers/ laptops is 
not part of institutional equipment but NCHE encourages HEIs to develop personal laptop 
policies to increase student computer access. Nevertheless, not all students may have the 
capacity to afford the laptops therefore NCHE encourages HEIs to have a stock of desktops 
for students’ access. Table 8.1 summarises student access to computers and books at higher

7.3  Open Distance e-Learning (ODeL)
In 2019/20, following closure of HEIs, NCHE developed an emergency ODeL programme 
to enable HEIs complete the academic year. Whereas this required well-developed ICT 
systems, well-equipped computer laboratories, robust servers and adequate bandwidth to 
withstand data transaction, a majority of HEIs were ill equipped to handle these demands. 
The HEIs did not provide comprehensive data on this however; the ODeL monitoring 
reports showed that at the universities level, almost all have robust servers, weak monitoring 
systems and inadequacy of computers. The bandwidth remains inadequate to handle the 
demands. Generally, staff were ill prepared to adopt ODeL mode of delivery because skills 
gaps. The HEIs adopted blended learning rather than pure ODeL. HEIs taught practicum 
areas physically while observing SOPs. The OTIs could not cope with ODeL requirements 
and have lagged behind. They lack equipment & facilities and face scarcity of skills to 
conduct ODeL.
7.4  Access to books
There has been increasing debate on e-resources versus hard copy textbooks and physical 
libraries verses virtual libraries. NCHE recommends that every HEIs must have a 
physical library and a stock of hard copy textbooks in their respective institutions. This is 
because bandwidth, access to; computers, internet, e-resources, and a conducive reading 
environment for students is still wanting. Many student do not have access to computers 
and dwell in noisy neighbourhoods that are not conducive for study. In 2019/20, the number 
of hard copy textbooks reported was 3,789,698 representing a decline by 17% in numbers 
reported in 2018/19. Among other reasons, this decline arises from institutions that have 
ceased to operate. The student book ratio also dropped from 16 to 14 per student irrespective 
of the titles. The theological institutions continue dominating highest access to hard copy 
textbooks student to book ratio of 1:67, followed by university at 1:17 respectively. Others 
categories are operating below acceptable levels according to NCHE quality assurance 
indicators.

8.0   Expenditure for Public and Private Univ ersities
8.1   Introduction
This subsection reports on financial health of HEIs institutional focusing mainly on budgets 
and respective budget performances. In 2019/20, a few higher education institutions that 
were responsive provided scanty data on this section and registered the worst performance 
in this area. These HEIs blamed poor performance on Covid-19 pandemic and presidential 
directive to close higher education institutions.
The data obtained from a few HEIs that obliged showed great deviations between 
planned before onset of the pandemic and actual budget realized. Many HEIs operated 
on emergency budgets to mitigate to challenges that arose from Covid-19 pandemic. 
Institutions that depended on donor and government funding for their operations had 
some of their funding suspended, while others that depended on tuition suffered a great 
financial shock. Other than public and a few private & church founded HEIs many could 
not sustain even payment of salaries for their staff. Most budgets remained under funded 
or completely unfunded yet they were set as priorities in respective budgets. In the wake 
of Covid-19 pandemic, HEIs and planners should rethink of alternative sustainable sources 
of funding if higher education is to stay sustainable.
